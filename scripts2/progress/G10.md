# G10 — Inverse solve: the global elevation-and-azimuth grid

Issue: #11 · Branch: `sprint/gnomon-mvp` · Status: **shipped**

## What shipped

`Gnomon.grid` — a brute-force sweep of longitude −180→180 at 0.5° and latitude
−90→90 at 0.25°, evaluating the solar position at every cell for a claimed UTC
instant and keeping those where the sun would have been where the shadows say.
519,120 evaluations in **404 ms**. 13 headless assertions; 171 total.

## The gate

**A band derived from a known real location and time contains that location.**
Sydney, 22 December 2023, 15:20 AEDT: the true elevation is 56.42° and Sydney's
own cell comes back at strength **1.000**. London, in darkness at that instant,
is excluded outright.

| filter | cells kept |
|---|---|
| elevation ±0.5° | 2,875 |
| elevation ±0.5° + azimuth ±2° | 43 |
| unbounded elevation | 259,560 |

Adding azimuth collapses the band to a patch, exactly as intended, and the true
location survives the second filter at strength 1.000.

## Decisions taken

- **Membership is a Gaussian, and the width comes from the band itself.** A
  p5–p95 interval is a 90% band, so its half-width is about 1.645σ if the
  distribution is roughly normal. Treating it that way gives the soft edge for
  free and from a real assumption rather than from a curve chosen to look nice.

- **An unbounded elevation admits the whole daylit world, not nothing.** "We
  cannot tell" must not silently render as an empty map, which reads as "nowhere
  on Earth fits" — the exact opposite of what it means. An unbounded azimuth
  band is simply not applied, with a test asserting the cell count is unchanged.

- **Angular comparisons take the shortest way round.** An azimuth band of
  355°–5° is 10° wide, not 350°. Both the filter and the half-width computation
  wrap, with a test on a band straddling the seam.

- **`strengthAt` recomputes rather than looking up the nearest cell.** Asking
  "is this place in the band?" should not inherit the grid's resolution as an
  error, so an arbitrary point is evaluated directly with the same arithmetic.

## Getting to sub-second, without a second implementation

The first working version took **1426 ms** — over the budget the whole design
rests on, since G12's scrubber has to redo this on every frame. Two changes took
it to 404 ms, and neither duplicates the solar maths:

1. **Per-latitude trig hoisted through a new `solar.row(delta, phi)`.** Sweeping
   asks the same question at 720 longitudes for each latitude, and the sines and
   cosines of declination and latitude are identical across all of them.
   `solar.look` is now written *in terms of* `row`, so the fast path and the
   ordinary one are the same arithmetic rather than a copy that can drift.
   Longitude enters only through the hour angle, so its sine and cosine are
   computed once per column.

2. **Cells are rejected on their geometric elevation before refraction is
   evaluated.** The refraction fit is the most expensive part of a cell, and the
   overwhelming majority of the world is nowhere near the band. Rejecting beyond
   `1.5 × halfWidth + 0.7°` is safe: 1.5 half-widths is where the Gaussian has
   already fallen below the 0.05 floor, and 0.7° is comfortably more than
   refraction's largest possible contribution.

There is a test asserting the grid agrees with `solar.position` cell for cell at
three scattered locations, which is what stops either optimisation drifting into
a separate implementation.

## Left for later slices

- G11 renders `cells` with their `strength` driving the soft edge. **The
  softness must come from that value, not be invented at draw time.**
- G12's scrubber re-runs `solve` per frame. At 404 ms that is a few frames a
  second — if it feels sluggish, **cache across frames rather than coarsening
  the grid**, because the resolution is what makes the band honest.
- G13's Verify mode wants `grid.at(context, lat, lon)` to get the predicted sun
  at a known place, then compares against the measured band.
